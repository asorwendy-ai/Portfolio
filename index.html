The Approach

A finding nobody closes is just a liability with a timestamp.

Security programs rarely fail at the analysis. They fail in the gap between identifying a risk and getting an organization to actually do something about it. That gap is a delivery problem, not a technical one. That is why I run remediation as sprints with owners, velocity, and a burn-down, rather than as a report I hand over and hope about.

Proportionate, not maximal

Every control I recommend has a documented justification, and so does every control I deliberately exclude. Blanket framework adoption is expensive theater. Scoping is the actual skill.

Evidence that generates itself

Manual control assessment decays the moment the audit ends. I build pipelines and scanning frameworks that produce continuous evidence, so audit readiness is a byproduct of shipping rather than a fire drill.

Risk in plain language

Executives don't accept risks they don't understand. They just ignore them. I write risk acceptances a non-technical decision-maker can actually sign, including what happens if we're wrong.

AI as a control, not a buzzword

I use AI to compress security work that used to take weeks, and I govern an AI system that makes consequential decisions about real people. Both sides of that, in production.

CASE 01 · FLAGSHIP
Governing a high-risk AI system that matches strangers for in-person meetings
Founding an AI product forced me to run a real AI risk assessment where the residual risk was mine to carry, while also owning the roadmap that shipped it.
GymSync AI · Founder, Technical Project Manager & Application Security Lead · Oct 2024 – Present
+
The problem

GymSync is an AI-powered social fitness platform that matches people with compatible workout partners and coordinates them into meeting in person. Most teams would scope this as a data-privacy problem. It isn't. An algorithm that recommends which stranger you should meet at a gym at 6am carries a risk surface that no SOC 2 report covers: physical safety, matching fairness across demographics, and the trust users extend to a recommendation they assume is neutral.

Constraints I was working under
  • Pre-revenue startup with no budget for a dedicated security hire, a GRC platform, or an external audit
  • Distributed engineering team on two-week Agile sprints against an app-store launch deadline
  • Two mobile platforms with different review regimes; an Apple rejection mid-cycle forced a feature-scope reduction
  • Consumer PII plus health-adjacent fitness data, triggering GDPR and CCPA obligations from day one
  • I was simultaneously the security function and the delivery function, which put competing incentives inside one person
What I did
  • Owned the full project lifecycle: defined the roadmap, ran two-week sprints with a distributed team, and delivered milestones ahead of schedule
  • Facilitated sprint planning, standups, retrospectives, and backlog refinement, holding a priority queue aligned to business objectives rather than to whoever asked loudest
  • Designed and enforced a secure SDLC with security testing embedded in the CI/CD pipeline, so scanning gated releases rather than trailing them
  • Built the privacy posture in as an architectural default: encryption at rest, IAM-scoped access controls, and secured API integration across service boundaries
  • Moved secrets to environment-based access policies enforced through AWS IAM, eliminating credential sprawl before it could accumulate
  • Treated the Apple rejection as a scope-reduction forcing function, shipping a tighter, more defensible feature set rather than contesting it
Outcome
60%
Reduction in code exposure risk
2
Regimes satisfied at launch (GDPR, CCPA)
0
Dedicated security headcount required
The trade-off I accepted

I chose architectural controls over documentation depth. With no compliance staff, formal artifacts (a full SoA, a maintained risk register, a model card for the matching algorithm) would have consumed the same hours as the encryption and IAM work. I made the encryption real first and accepted that the paperwork lagged. That's a defensible call at pre-launch scale and an indefensible one at Series A, which is exactly when it gets addressed.

Why this matters for the role

Most AI governance candidates have written policy about systems someone else built. I've been the person deciding whether a feature ships when the risk assessment is uncomfortable and the runway is finite, and then running the sprint that shipped it. That changes how you write governance. You stop producing controls that sound rigorous and start producing controls a team will actually follow.

CASE 02
Turning audit readiness from a fire drill into a pipeline output
Embedding security testing into CI/CD at a global pharmaceutical company, where a failed audit is a regulatory event rather than an inconvenience.
Novartis · Information Systems Security Analyst · Jan 2022 – Mar 2024
+
The problem

Security assessment was happening downstream of development. Vulnerabilities surfaced in production, control evidence was assembled by hand ahead of each audit cycle, and system security documentation drifted out of sync with what was actually deployed. In a regulated pharma environment, that gap between documented and actual state is the finding.

Constraints I was working under
  • DevOps teams measured on delivery velocity, with no incentive to absorb security friction
  • FedRAMP and ISO 27001 alignment required, across systems mid-migration to cloud
  • Three organizations (DevOps, Compliance, and Engineering) with no shared definition of done
  • Legacy documentation covering systems whose architecture had already moved on
What I did
  • Coordinated cross-functional security projects across DevOps, Compliance, and Engineering, tracking milestones, resolving blockers, and reporting status to senior leadership
  • Integrated SAST, DAST, and container image scanning directly into CI/CD pipelines, shifting detection left of deployment
  • Partnered with DevOps to automate security control assessments rather than mandating them. The control ran as part of the build, so compliance stopped competing with velocity
  • Authored and maintained 20+ System Security Plans and POA&Ms aligned to FedRAMP and ISO 27001, tied to actual deployed state
  • Supported security testing and validation through cloud migration, where the risk profile changed underneath existing controls
Outcome
40%
Reduction in production vulnerabilities
30%
Improvement in audit readiness
20+
SSPs and POA&Ms authored and maintained
The trade-off I accepted

Automating control assessment meant accepting the scanners' coverage limits. SAST and DAST catch known classes well and miss business-logic flaws almost entirely. I took the consistency and the continuous evidence over broader-but-sporadic manual review, on the reasoning that a control running every build beats a thorough one running twice a year. That gap is real, though, and needs manual threat modeling layered on top.

CASE 03
Fixing the SLA problem nobody thought was a security problem
Vulnerability and access risk management across 50+ enterprise platforms, where the root cause turned out to live in change management, not the scanner.
Bristol Myers Squibb · Cybersecurity Analyst (Contract) · Jan 2021 – Nov 2021
+
The problem

Vulnerability scanning across a hybrid cloud estate was producing findings faster than remediation could close them, and SLA compliance was suffering. The instinct was to tune the scanners. The actual failure was upstream: access provisioning and change management ran on separate workflows, so remediation work stalled waiting on access that nobody owned granting.

Constraints I was working under
  • Hybrid infrastructure spanning on-premise and Azure, with inconsistent tooling coverage
  • 50+ enterprise platforms under different business owners with competing priorities
  • Three scanning tools (Tenable, Qualys, Rapid7) producing overlapping and sometimes contradictory findings
What I did
  • Deployed and operated Tenable and Rapid7 across hybrid cloud infrastructure, reconciling output against Qualys to produce a single defensible finding set
  • Performed vulnerability analysis and access risk reviews across 50+ enterprise platforms, prioritizing by exploitability and business exposure rather than raw CVSS
  • Traced the SLA failure to the provisioning/change-management seam and aligned the two workflows so remediation wasn't blocked on access
  • Investigated Azure cloud misconfigurations and drove remediation with the owning teams
  • Authored compliance checklists and enforced application security controls across SDLC pipelines
Outcome
85%
Improvement in SLA compliance
50+
Enterprise platforms under review
What I'd carry forward

The lesson wasn't about tooling. When a security metric is failing, the cause is frequently a process seam outside security's org chart, and the fastest fix is often to go negotiate with the team that owns the other half rather than to buy another scanner.

CASE 04
Running security remediation as sprints, not as a report
Right-sizing NIST RMF for a healthcare provider that couldn't afford all of it, then using Scrum to make sure the findings actually closed.
Naioth in Ramah Counseling Services · Lead Cybersecurity Consultant & Technical Project Lead · Mar 2024 – Present
+
The problem

A healthcare counseling practice holds some of the most sensitive data categories that exist, under HIPAA, with none of the security budget of the pharma companies I'd come from. Applying NIST RMF at full scope would have produced a beautiful assessment the organization could not act on. Two tasks, then. Find the subset of controls that genuinely moved risk. Then, harder, make sure the resulting findings didn't die in a document.

Constraints I was working under
  • Small healthcare provider with no internal security function and no GRC tooling budget
  • HIPAA obligations over highly sensitive patient records
  • Clinical staff as primary users, with near-zero tolerance for controls that add friction to care delivery
  • No dedicated remediation team, so closure depended on people whose actual job was something else
What I did
  • Led security reviews of 15+ systems using NIST RMF, scoped to the controls with real risk-reduction value at this size
  • Served as Scrum lead for security remediation sprints, facilitating standups, maintaining backlogs in JIRA, and tracking sprint velocity to drive on-time closure of high-risk findings
  • Managed end-to-end project timelines, stakeholder updates, and delivery checkpoints across all engagements
  • Designed secure Infrastructure-as-Code templates using Ansible and AWS CloudFormation, making the hardened configuration the default deployment path
  • Ran misconfiguration scans across AWS workloads and drove resolution of 50+ high-risk issues
  • Built an automated API security scanning framework via Jenkins, so monitoring continued after my engagement rather than ending with it
Outcome
15+
Systems reviewed under NIST RMF
50+
High-risk AWS issues resolved
40%
Reduction in manual testing overhead
The trade-off I accepted

I deliberately did not implement the full control set, and I documented why for each exclusion. Recommending controls a client cannot staff produces a report that gets filed and ignored, which leaves them less safe than an honest, smaller program they'll actually run. Proportionate compliance is a defensible position, but only if the exclusions are written down and the residual risk is formally accepted by someone with the authority to accept it.

CASE 05 · FOUNDATION
Learning that access control is where audits are actually won
ITGC audits under SOX and COBIT, the grounding in evidence, walkthroughs, and IAM findings that shapes how I build controls now.
Lamda IT Consulting Services Inc. · Junior IT Auditor · Jan 2020 – Dec 2020
+
The problem

IT general controls audits under SOX and COBIT, across business systems where access had accumulated for years without systematic review. The recurring pattern: organizations had documented access policies, and separately had access reality, and nobody had reconciled the two in a long time.

What I did
  • Led ITGC audits and application access control reviews under SOX and COBIT frameworks, surfacing and driving mitigation of 20+ high-risk IAM findings
  • Managed walkthroughs and evidence collection across 10+ business systems against enterprise access standards
  • Developed and maintained Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery plans in line with NIST SP 800-34
Outcome
20+
High-risk IAM findings mitigated
10+
Business systems audited
Why it still matters

Starting on the audit side means I design controls already knowing what evidence they'll need to produce and how an assessor will test them. Engineers who've never sat on the other side of a walkthrough tend to build controls that work but can't be proven, and unprovable controls fail audits regardless of whether they're effective.
